Abstract

Understanding microclimate variation along elevation gradients is critical for predicting species distribution shifts under climate change. We deployed 147 temperature and humidity sensors at 50-meter elevation intervals along six trail corridors in the Oregon Cascades (elevation range 450-2,180 m) over three consecutive years (2021-2023). Mean annual temperature decreased linearly at 5.8 °C per 1,000 m of elevation gain (R² = 0.94), consistent with the standard environmental lapse rate. However, we found significant seasonal deviation: summer lapse rates (June-August) averaged 7.2 °C/km, while winter rates (December-February) averaged only 4.1 °C/km, likely due to cold air pooling in valley bottoms and thermal inversions. Relative humidity showed an inverse relationship, increasing 8.3% per 1,000 m. Trail corridors created measurably different microclimates compared to closed-canopy forest at the same elevation, with trail-edge sensors recording 2.1 °C higher mean summer maxima and 11% lower relative humidity. These findings have implications for trail management, restoration ecology, and species range modeling in mountainous terrain.

Keywords: microclimate, elevation gradient, lapse rate, trail ecology, Pacific Northwest, species distribution, climate change, forest management

1. Introduction

Mountain environments exhibit steep environmental gradients over short horizontal distances, creating a mosaic of microclimates that support exceptional biodiversity (Körner, 2007). The relationship between elevation and temperature, commonly expressed as the environmental lapse rate, is fundamental to ecological modeling, yet local factors including aspect, canopy cover, topographic position, and proximity to water bodies can cause substantial deviation from free-atmosphere predictions (Dobrowski, 2011; Lenoir et al., 2017).

Of particular interest to land managers is the microclimate effect of trail corridors themselves. Trails create linear canopy gaps that alter light, temperature, humidity, and wind exposure at the forest edge (Marion et al., 2016). As recreational trail use in the Pacific Northwest has increased 47% since 2015 (USFS, 2023), understanding the ecological footprint of trail infrastructure has become increasingly urgent.

2. Methods

We selected six trail corridors in the Willamette and Deschutes National Forests spanning a range of aspects, forest types, and trail widths. At each corridor, we installed paired sensor stations (HOBO MX2301A, Onset Computer Corporation) at 50 m elevation intervals: one at the trail edge (within 1 m of tread) and one at 30 m perpendicular distance into closed-canopy forest. Sensors logged temperature and relative humidity at 15-minute intervals from June 2021 through September 2023.

4. Discussion

The elevated summer lapse rate (7.2 °C/km vs. the annual mean of 5.8 °C/km) suggests that high-elevation sites experience disproportionately greater warming during the growing season. This has direct implications for species at their upper range limits, where even small temperature increases may shift competitive dynamics. The trail-edge effect, while modest in magnitude, extends at least 5 m into the forest interior and creates corridors of altered microclimate that could facilitate range shifts for thermophilic species.

5. Conclusions

Our three-year dataset demonstrates that elevation-dependent microclimate variation in Pacific Northwest trail corridors is both larger and more seasonally variable than standard lapse-rate models predict. Trail management plans should account for the measurable microclimate alteration that trail infrastructure introduces. Future work should examine whether trail-edge microclimates serve as dispersal corridors for low-elevation species moving upslope under climate change.
